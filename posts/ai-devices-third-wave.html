The third wave of AI devices

Smart speakers taught us to talk to computers. AI pins taught us what not to build. Now that agents can take on whole tasks, a crop of rings, glasses, headsets and boxes is being built so you can hand one over at any moment, wherever you are.

Part One

The big picture

Why a category that flopped two years ago is suddenly crowded again.

Why AI devices are back

On November 3 a two-person Y Combinator startup called Cyclon announced it is building "an AI phone to replace your iPhone." The launch post drew more than 700,000 views.

It is the latest in a run of launches that would have been hard to imagine eighteen months ago, when the Humane AI Pin had just been switched off. In the past four weeks alone there has been a $3,499 box whose only job is to run your AI agents at home, a $99 ring that summons whichever agent you use, and glasses that turn silently mouthed words into text.

The obvious reaction is skepticism. We have been here before, and every earlier wave was also about handing something to an assistant: Alexa set a timer, the AI Pin answered a question. What has changed is the size of the job. Agents can now take on long-running tasks that are closer to goals than commands: find a table for Friday and tell everyone, chase the overdue invoice, plan the trip. As they have become more capable, people have become more comfortable handing over the whole job rather than one step of it. That is the thread through this piece: the third wave is about making sure there is always a way to hand over a task, at any moment of the day, wherever you are, with as little friction as possible. Part Two

Three waves

Part One claimed this wave is different. To see why, start with the two that came before it. Figure 2 shows all three at a glance; the sections after it explain what each one learned.

Wave 1: the smart speaker

The first mainstream AI device was a cylinder in the kitchen. Voice got three things right: it took the screen out of simple jobs like timers and music, it put a computer in a shared room that the whole family could use without a login, and it made the home a place where software lived all day.

Then voice stalled. The assistants matched what you said to a fixed list of commands, and anything off the list got an apology. Nobody could remember which third-party "skills" existed, use settled into a handful of jobs, and the voice shopping that was meant to pay for cheap hardware never took off. The lesson was not that voice was wrong, but that voice with a shallow brain behind it tops out quickly.

Voice stalled for want of a brain. When large language models supplied one, a new set of companies tried again.

Wave 2: the first AI gadgets

Many concluded the phone was about to be displaced. Humane's AI Pin was the ambitious bet: a clip-on with a camera, a cellular connection and a laser that projected a menu onto your palm. Reviewers found it slow, hot and short on battery, with answers that were often wrong. Within a year HP had bought Humane's assets for less than the company had raised, and the pins stopped working. Rabbit's R1 was cheaper and more playful, pitched on a "large action model" that would operate apps for you. At launch it could do far less than promised, and it became shorthand for an AI gadget nobody needed.

Both made the same two mistakes. They asked people to carry a second device that did less than the first, at a premium. And each built its own device, operating system and assistant on top of licensed models, instead of plugging into the assistants people already used, which were improving faster than any hardware company could keep up with.

Both Wave 2 flagships failed. A quieter category didn't.

The lull, and who survived

What survived the shakeout was a set of narrow recorders: Bee's wristband, Limitless's pendant and Plaud's card that sticks to the back of a phone. Amazon bought Bee and Meta bought Limitless. Plaud stayed independent.

The pattern was one job, cheap hardware and a subscription behind it. Big tech bought the teams, Amazon to feed Alexa and Meta to feed its glasses, and learned what the startups had: capture is only half of it. The value is in what happens to the captured context next.

Part Three

What this wave is betting on

Part Two ended with the lull, and its lesson that capture is only half the job. Wave 3, the interfaces for agents in Figure 2, takes on the other half: acting on what you want. This part sorts those devices by the bet each one is making and by where it sits, from your finger to your living room.

The bets, grouped by idea

Most Wave 3 devices are small, cheap, made by teams of two or three, and built to sit next to the phone rather than replace it. Figure 3 maps all of them from your body outward, and a pattern shows: the closer a device sits to you, the less it does on its own and the more it leans on something else. The buttons at the top of the figure pick out the five bets the devices fall into.

1. New ways to give input

Voice is the obvious input for AI, and it fails in exactly the places people spend their days: open offices, trains, cafés. So one group is trying to keep the speed of speech without the sound. Subvocal's glasses use radio waves to read your mouth as you silently form words; Dydema's headset reads brain signals and binds a chosen thought to a command. The big companies are making the same bet. In January Apple paid a reported close to $2 billion for Q.ai, which reads silent speech from the skin of your face. Meta is coming at the same problem from a different angle: instead of silent speech, the wristband that ships with its display glasses reads the muscle signals in your wrist, so a pinch or a letter traced with your finger stands in for a spoken command.

2. A remote control for your agents

The largest group assumes the agent already exists and builds the button that summons it, as in Figure 1. Natura's $99 ring sends what you say to whichever agent you choose. Aina's Dune puts three agent keys on your desk, Vexo's bracelet listens for context and taps your wrist when something needs you, and antimattr pairs a ring with earphones built to hear only your voice. These devices are less about capturing your life than about directing work.

3. Capture and memory

The descendants of Bee, Plaud and Limitless still remember for you, and the competition has moved to form factor, audio quality and what the software does with the transcript. Pocket snaps onto your phone and lets other agents read your conversations. Sandbar's ring records only while you press it. Nirva's pendant is the clearest case of capture recast as self-knowledge: it tracks your mood, your energy and the people you spend time with.

4. Local, private compute

If agents are going to read your email, files and messages around the clock, some people want that to happen on a machine they own. Ghost Core is a $3,499 screenless box that runs open models, and the agents that use them, at home with no subscription. Lucebox sells a bigger machine to developers and small teams.

5. A phone with no apps to tap

Cyclon is the boldest bet of the set: an operating system that works inside your existing apps with your own accounts, and builds small apps on demand when the one you need doesn't exist.

Of the four zones in Figure 3, one has the giants' full attention.

The home, round two

All three giants are back in the living room. Amazon rebuilt Alexa around a large language model, opened it to every US customer in February, and says people now talk to it two to three times as often. Google put Gemini into its first new speaker in six years. OpenAI's first device, designed with Jony Ive's team, is reportedly a screenless speaker with a camera, due in early 2027.

Why the home, again? Because it fixes most of what is hard about wearables. A speaker has wall power, so it can listen and compute all day. It is shared, so one device serves a family and the agent can learn a household rather than a person. It sits next to the cameras, locks and thermostats that agents can actually act on. And Amazon and Google already have hundreds of millions of devices in place, which means distribution is solved. The first round was lost to shallow intelligence. With agents behind them, the same cylinders get a second chance, and this time the competitor is not another speaker but whichever agent the household already uses on its phones.

Rings, phones, boxes and speakers all promise a new interface. Before going further, it's worth pinning down what that means.

What a "new interface" actually means

It is easy to file all of this under "AI gadgets." The better question is how each differs from the three interfaces we already have: the phone, the voice assistant and the chatbot. Figure 4 sets each one against Wave 3.

The common thread is that a new interface reduces the attention cost of delegating. The phone made information instant but demands your eyes and thumbs for every step. A good Wave 3 device takes seconds of your attention for minutes of work, as in Figure 1, which is why so many of them have no screen.

Part Four

Why now

Part Three ended on what makes Wave 3 a new interface: seconds of your attention for minutes of an agent's work. That only works if the agent is capable. This part explains why, in 2026, it finally is.

The agentic shift

The single biggest reason this wave exists is that, for the first time, there is something useful on the other end of the button. Three launches from the past three months show the shape of it.

Grok Bot, from SpaceX's AI division (formerly xAI), arrived in beta on August 11. It gives you a roster of persistent agents that run on a cloud computer, sign into the apps and sites you already use, including ones with no clean API, and work through multi-step jobs until they need your approval. For now it is limited to the top SuperGrok and Cursor plans.

Muse, Meta's personal agent, launched on September 8 and went to the top of Apple's App Store. It connects to email, calendars and payments, runs in its own virtual machine in Meta's cloud, and has a Mac app that operates the apps on your computer. It is free with metered usage, with paid plans at $20 and $100 a month.

Instinct, a private-beta assistant from former Sierra researcher Noah Shinn, which has reportedly since raised $250 million, impressed early testers with how much it could get done on its own. It also alarmed them: its first terms of service claimed sweeping rights over users' data, which Instinct rewrote within days. More on that in Part Five.

Once an agent can do the work, a device doesn't need to be smart. It needs to be there.

That is what turns a device into more than a voice remote. A Wave 1 speaker could only do what its own assistant could do. A Natura ring or an Aina keypad is a front end to whichever agent you trust, and it gets more useful every time that agent improves, without a hardware update. The device makers get to ride the agent companies' progress instead of competing with it, which is the opposite of the position Humane put itself in by building its own assistant.

Agents explain why the devices can be simple. Four changes underneath explain why they can exist at all.

The tech underneath

On-device and at-home models got good enough. A graphics card with 24GB of its own memory can now run an open-weight model of around 30 billion parameters, which is enough for Ghost to run an agent at home without a data center. Even a $99 Google speaker now ships with a neural processing unit. Local models are still behind frontier models in the cloud, so the realistic architecture for most devices is hybrid: wake words, transcription and private memory on the device, heavy reasoning in the cloud.

New sensors made silent input possible. The input devices in Part Three each read a different signal: radio waves bouncing off the mouth, electrical signals from wrist muscles (EMG), brain activity at the scalp (EEG), the skin of the face. What they share is a dependence on training data. A decoder trained on one person tends to fail on the next, so the winners will be whoever collects the most varied labeled signals. Meta says its band draws on research with close to 200,000 participants, and Dydema's whole strategy is to be the dataset. It is the same bottleneck I wrote about in Where robot training data comes from: when the input is physical, data collection is the moat.

Rings and pendants got cheap. Oura and its imitators built the supply chain for tiny, waterproof, all-day hardware. That is why Natura can sell a ring with health sensors and a microphone for $99, and why a three-person YC team can ship a bracelet.

Battery life stopped being the deal-breaker, but only for devices that do little on board. Natura's ring lasts six to twelve days; Meta's display glasses last about six hours. The less a device computes and shows, the longer it lasts, which is another argument for pushing the thinking to the phone, the home box or the cloud.

Part Five

Go-to-market

Part Four explained why these devices can exist now. Existing isn't the same as selling, so this part covers what it takes to win a buyer and keep the device in use.

Picking the wedge

Every device that has survived so far started with one job good enough to earn a place on your body or in your home. Plaud's job is meetings. Sandbar's is catching a thought before it disappears. Aina's is the shortcut you hit fifty times a day. The Echo's was the kitchen timer.

A good wedge has three properties. It happens many times a day, so the device builds a habit. It is clearly better than reaching for the phone, in speed, discretion or hands-free use. And it produces something the user values on its own, such as a transcript or a finished task, so the device is worth keeping even before the bigger vision arrives.

A wedge earns the first purchase. The business model decides whether the company survives it.

Pricing and business model

Two models are emerging, with a third borrowed from big tech.

The model choice is really about who pays for inference. An always-listening device generates minutes of audio to transcribe and summarize every hour, and an agent that runs in the background burns tokens while you sleep. Plaud gives buyers 300 free minutes a month and makes its money when heavy users go past them, which is why close to half of its users pay. Natura plans to start charging after an initial free period for the same reason. Ghost flips the equation: at $3,499 the box costs about as much as fourteen years of a $20-a-month chatbot plan, but once you own it, running it costs only electricity. The founders who get this right will treat compute cost per active user as a core product metric, not a finance line.

Even with the right wedge and the right price, every device runs into the same gatekeepers.

The platform problem

Every one of these devices leans on a phone, and Apple and Google decide what the phone lets it do. On an iPhone, an outside app can't freely keep a microphone open in the background or reach into your other apps, so a ring's answer comes back through your headphones or a lock-screen notification. Agents hit the same wall: Muse can operate the apps on a Mac, but whether it can ever work inside the apps on your iPhone is Apple's call, not Meta's. Cyclon's answer is to ship its own operating system, which gives it reach no iPhone app has, at the cost of depending on every app behaving when a bot drives it.

The platform owners can also copy what works. A day after Subvocal announced its funding, a developer built a rough AirPods version of its glasses in nine minutes, and Apple has already bought Q.ai. A startup's best defense is to go where the platforms are slow, with new sensors, new form factors and agents that work across platforms, and to own a job before the platform notices it.

The platform decides what a device is allowed to do. Trust decides what users will let it do.

Trust and privacy

This wave asks for more trust than any consumer technology before it, and it asks in two places. The device decides when you are heard. The agent decides what it can touch. Devices differ a lot on the first. On the second there is little choice: an agent that takes on whole tasks has to read your accounts and act in your name, so the real question is how safely it does both. Figure 6 takes each in turn.

Always-on listening will split the market, and regulators and workplaces will have opinions about it. On the agent side, the companies that win trust will be the ones that make permissions narrow, approvals visible and revocation instant, and say all of it in plain language.

Trust is the newest test these devices face. The older ones were set by the last wave, which failed most of them.

Lessons from Wave 2

Six lessons for anyone building in this wave. Each pairs the Wave 2 mistake that taught it with a Wave 3 device built around it.

01

Complement the phone

Wave 2

Humane and Rabbit asked people to carry a second device that did less than the first.

Wave 3

Natura's ring and Plaud's recorder lean on the phone you already carry for the screen, the connection and the apps.

02

One job, shown honestly

Wave 2

The AI Pin promised calls, texts, translation, photos and answers, and reviewers found it did most of them poorly. The R1 did far less at launch than its keynote promised.

Wave 3

Every survivor so far started with one job good enough to earn its place, from the Echo's kitchen timer to Plaud's meetings. The rest came later.

03

Borrow the brain

Wave 2

Both built their own device, operating system and assistant, instead of plugging into the ones people already used, which improved faster than any hardware company could keep up with.

Wave 3

Natura sends your request to the agent you pick: Claude, ChatGPT, Grok Bot, Muse or Instinct. It gets better every time they do.

04

Respect the physics

Wave 2

Reviewers found the AI Pin slow, hot and short on battery. If the answer takes longer than pulling out a phone, the phone wins.

Wave 3

Natura's ring lasts six to twelve days on a charge, because it does almost nothing on board and leaves the thinking to the agent.

05

Price for a trial

Wave 2

$699 up front plus $24 a month, asking for conviction before the product had earned it.

Wave 3

Rings at $99, and Plaud's 300 free minutes a month: cheap to try, with heavy users paying for the AI they consume.

06

Outlive your own company

Wave 2

When HP bought Humane's assets, every AI Pin stopped working. Owners were left with a clip-on that did nothing. Now every buyer of startup hardware asks what happens if the company folds.

Wave 3

Ghost says its Core keeps working if the company disappears, because the software and models live on the box. That answer is now part of the pitch.

Part Six

What comes next

Part Five covered how a launch is won. The last part asks where the category ends up.

Where it's heading

The central question is whether this ends as a phone companion or a phone replacement.

The near-term answer is companion. Almost every device in this piece, from Natura's ring to Ghost's box, assumes your phone stays in your pocket, and leans on it for connectivity, display and identity. That is the lesson of Wave 2 applied correctly.

The long-term answer depends on what the giants do. Three moves are worth watching. Apple bought Q.ai, which points to silent input for AirPods and future glasses, and to Apple absorbing the best ideas of this wave into products people already own. OpenAI is starting in the home with a screenless speaker, with a phone reportedly further out, which is the clearest sign that an AI company believes it needs its own hardware to avoid living on someone else's platform. Meta is assembling the most complete stack of anyone: display glasses, an EMG wristband, the Limitless team and a personal agent in Muse that it plans to bring to those glasses.

If any of them makes the agent the primary interface, so that you mostly ask and confirm rather than open and tap, the phone becomes a screen you consult rather than a place you live. Cyclon is betting that shift happens soon enough for a startup to ride it. The more likely path is slower: the phone stays, the apps fade into the background, and the interface moves out onto the body and into the room.

Bottom line

The first wave let us hand a computer one command from across the room. The second tried to take on more, with a capable model trapped in hardware that couldn't carry it. The third arrives as agents become able to take on whole tasks, close to goals, and keep working on them long after you have stopped thinking about them. That shifts the device's job from being smart to being there: small, cheap and always within reach whenever you want to hand something over.

That makes this wave easier to start and harder to win. Building a ring is no longer the hard part. The hard parts are picking a job people will repeat many times a day, covering the cost of the AI behind it, getting enough access to the phone to be useful, and earning the trust to listen and act. The companies that get those four right will not replace the phone. They will make it something you reach for a lot less often.

Part Seven

Reference

The technical terms used throughout, in one place.

Glossary

Agent
AI software that can take actions on your behalf, such as signing into apps, filling in forms and sending messages, rather than only answering questions.
Agentic
Describes a product built around agents doing multi-step work with limited supervision.
Always listening
A device whose microphone stays on so it can capture conversations without being triggered, as opposed to push-to-talk.
Connector
A built-in link that lets an agent read from or act in a specific service, such as email or a calendar.
EEG
Electroencephalography: reading electrical activity of the brain through sensors on the scalp. Used by non-invasive brain interfaces such as Dydema's.
EMG
Electromyography: reading the electrical signals of muscles. Meta's Neural Band reads wrist muscles to detect finger movements.
Inference
Running a trained model to produce an answer. Every transcript, query and agent step costs some, which is why someone has to pay for it.
Large action model
Rabbit's term for a model that operates software interfaces on a user's behalf; an early version of what agents now do.
MCP
Model Context Protocol: an open standard that lets AI apps connect to tools and data sources. Pocket exposes one so other agents can read your recordings.
NPU
Neural processing unit: a chip, or part of one, designed to run AI models efficiently on a device.
On-device AI
AI processing that happens on the gadget itself, which improves speed and privacy but is limited by power and memory.
Open-weight model
A model whose trained parameters are published, so anyone can run it on their own hardware.
Silent speech
Communicating with a computer by mouthing words or forming them internally without making sound.
Wedge
The first, narrow job a product does well enough to earn a place in someone's life, before it expands.
